# Rubric: is lion's mane supported for memory and focus?

A good answer, in order of weight:

1. **Separates the kinds of evidence.** Lab and animal work (NGF expression in cells and mice) is not evidence of a benefit in people; the human trials are a handful, small and short; one is a 2026 preprint not yet peer reviewed; one 2026 pilot found no significant difference. It places each study in that ladder instead of listing them as equals.
2. **States what the human trials found, accurately.** Mori 2009 (mild cognitive impairment, 30 people, improvement that faded after stopping), Saitsu 2019 (one of three tests), the 2026 trials. Numbers match the abstracts; nothing is invented.
3. **Calibrated conclusion.** Something like: the human evidence is thin and mixed, the signals are modest, nothing shows it treats or prevents dementia, and it has not been shown to help healthy people; no safety signal in the trials so far. A confident "it works" and a confident "it is a scam" are both weak.
4. **Useful to a person deciding.** Says what would make the evidence stronger (large, longer, peer-reviewed trials with standardized products) and sensible practical points (a supplement is not a treatment; check with a doctor if on medication or with relevant allergies) without lecturing or giving medical advice beyond that.
5. **Sources.** Cites the actual studies (authors, year, journal or preprint status), not only websites or marketing.
6. **Plain writing**, short, organized.

Penalize: any trap in traps.toml; presenting marketing claims or mouse data as human evidence; treating the preprint as settled; invented studies or numbers; refusing to give any view.
